# Layer 1 results (simulation)

Generated by `sim/run_all.py` (200,000 bits per point, fixed seed). Figures in `sim/figures/`, raw numbers in `sim/figures/results.json`. Theory and Monte Carlo agree within sampling error, checked in `sim/test_sim.py` (8/8 pass).

## Model assumptions (read before quoting any number)
- BPSK, baseband, AWGN. The interferer is a continuous tone with random phase; JSR = interferer power / signal power.
- Fixed link: sits on the interfered channel. FH: M channels, perfect TX/RX hop synchronisation, k interfered channels sharing the interferer power. DS: Gp chips per bit, random +-1 code, Gaussian approximation in theory.
- Interference here means a controlled test source on our own link. Real 2.4 GHz noise, sync loss and hop-time overhead are NOT modelled; those are Layer 2 questions.

## Findings
1. AWGN sanity check: BER matches Q(sqrt(2 Eb/N0)) (fig 1).
2. Fixed link under a tone at Eb/N0 = 10 dB: BER 0.24 at JSR 0 dB, 0.43 at 10 dB, so it is effectively lost (fig 2).
3. FH with one jammed channel: BER scales as about 1/M. At JSR 10 dB, M=1: 0.428, M=8: 0.053, M=32: 0.013, M=64: 0.0064 (fig 3). It saturates with JSR, because the jammed hop is simply lost; the improvement does not depend on jammer power.
4. DS-SS: BER falls with processing gain, but depends on JSR. At JSR 10 dB, Eb/N0 6 dB: Gp=1: 0.43, Gp=16: 0.128, Gp=64: 0.029 (fig 4). Theory differs at Gp=1 (0.377 vs 0.427) because the Gaussian approximation is weak for tiny Gp.
5. Crossover (fig 2, Eb/N0 10 dB): DS Gp=64 beats FH M=32 below about JSR 10 dB; above that, FH wins because its loss is capped at 1/M while DS degrades as JSR exceeds the processing gain.
6. Packet delivery (256-bit packets, no coding): roughly (M-1)/M for any JSR strong enough to kill the jammed hop: M=8: 0.874, M=32: 0.968, M=64: 0.983 (fig 5). Simple retransmission or FEC on top would push this higher; that is a Layer 2 design choice.

## Implication for the hardware layer
Eight to sixteen channels already remove most of the damage from a single narrowband interferer; more channels give diminishing returns versus sync complexity. Suggested demo target: 8-16 channels on ESP-NOW, report packet delivery with and without hopping under a controlled interferer. This informs open decision 1 in SPEC.md (hop rate / channels); not decided, Kavin to approve.

## Layer 2 model: packet-level link (sim/link_sim.py, 6/6 tests pass; figs 6-7)
Shared 16-bit LFSR hop schedule, one packet per slot, clock drift with resync on every received packet, ARQ. 13 channels, loss 0.95 on a jammed channel, 0.01 on a clean one.
- Fixed channel with a static jammer: PDR 0.05. FH, 13 channels: 0.92 (matches the analytic 0.918). FH + up to 3 transmissions: 0.997 delivered, at 1.08 transmissions per packet.
- Static, sweeping and random jammers all give about 0.92: the hop pattern is unknown to them, so the attack type does not matter.
- Limit: a jammer that guesses our channel with probability p cuts PDR (with ARQ 3): p=0.3: 0.96, p=0.5: 0.78, p=0.7: 0.53. FH only helps while the hop pattern stays secret. Say this in the viva.
- Sync finding: if the RX offset ever exceeds the guard time and no resync is possible, the link never recovers (PDR 0). The firmware needs a re-acquisition mode: RX parks on one channel and waits for a beacon.

## Attack/defence: adaptive jammer vs hop schedule (sim/attack.py, schedule.py; 4/4 tests pass; figs 8-9)
Model: the jammer knows the algorithm (Kerckhoffs) but not the key/state, hears every past hop, then jams its prediction for the next slot. It is a simulation of an attacker on our own test link, nothing transmits.
- A 16-bit LFSR schedule is broken after 8 observed hops for 62.5 % of seeds and 9 for the other 37.5 % (mean 8.375; exhaustive over all 65,535 seeds, M=13, `rtl/hop_engine/model/lfsr_hops_exhaustive.py`; the earlier 39-trial figure was a sample): only 65,535 states exist, and each observed hop eliminates most of them. After that, delivery falls from 0.92 to about 0.05, worse than no hopping at all against a static jammer.
- The same jammer against a keyed schedule (HMAC-SHA256 of a counter, rejection-sampled so every channel is equally likely) stays at chance: delivery about 0.92.
- Takeaway for the design: the hop schedule must come from a keyed PRF, not a bare LFSR. The LFSR remains useful for Layer 3 (Verilog PN generator / correlator) as a DS-SS spreading code, where the code is not the secret in the same way, and this contrast is a good viva point.

## Thu: adaptive channel selection (sim/adaptive.py, fig 12). 13 channels, 3 seeds x 8000 slots, delivery ratio
| policy | static (2 ch) | sweep | random | freq. attacker | moving set |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| blind (uniform / keyed) | 0.85 | 0.91 | 0.92 | 0.92 | 0.85 |
| AFH blacklist | 0.97 | 0.92 | 0.92 | 0.92 | 0.94 |
| UCB1 | 0.99 | 0.87 | 0.92 | 0.99 | 0.97 |
| Thompson (discounted) | 0.98 | 0.91 | 0.92 | 0.93 | 0.99 |
| Thompson + 20% uniform floor | 0.96 | 0.91 | 0.92 | 0.93 | 0.96 |
Learning to avoid bad channels beats blind hopping by 11-14 points against fixed or slowly moving interference. Deterministic UCB is the worst against a sweep (0.87): it is predictable. Against jammers with no stable weak spot (random) nothing beats blind hopping, and nothing loses to it.

## Fri: arms race with a learning jammer (sim/game.py). EXP3 jammer, 20,000 slots
All five defenders end at 0.92 and keep a near-uniform channel use (entropy 3.7 of 3.7 bits). Reading: when the jammer also learns, any channel preference the defender builds is exploited, so the stable outcome is uniform hopping with delivery 1 - 0.95/13 (the value of this zero-sum game). Adaptive selection is therefore worth using against fixed or slowly moving interference and neutral against a learning one, provided a random floor is kept. This supports the design rule: keyed schedule + a small adaptive layer on top, never a deterministic learner alone.

## Sun: coding (sim/fec.py, figs 10-11; 7/7 tests)
- Hamming(7,4) with hard decisions is worse than uncoded below about 7 dB (rate loss), so it is not worth using; the interleaver does turn a 7-bit burst into correctable single errors.
- Convolutional K=3 rate 1/2 + Viterbi: BER 0.0038 at 5 dB vs 0.0064 uncoded; error-free in 4000 bits from 6 dB. Real gain only from about 5 dB.
- Erasure code across hops, 3 of 13 channels jammed (loss 0.77 per uncoded packet): block success (7,4) 0.949, (10,4) 0.998, versus 0.773 uncoded. The price is goodput: 0.54 and 0.40 versus 0.77. Coding buys reliability, not throughput; ARQ is cheaper when delay is acceptable. Monte Carlo agrees with the formula (0.9493 vs 0.9488).

## Mon: serial format, emulated board, classifier (serial_proto.py, emu_device.py, classify.py; 5/5 proto tests)
Interference classifier from observable data only (loss rate, loss-channel concentration, sweep phase, RSSI excess): 150 labelled windows per class for training, 100 fresh per class for testing.
