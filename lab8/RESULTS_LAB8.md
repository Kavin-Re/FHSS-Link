Run: `python3 e12_placement.py` ... `python3 e17_moving.py` (each prints KEY NUMBER lines; seconds each, e16 about 2 min), `python3 test_lab8.py` (14 assertion tests, about 3 min; `python3 test_lab8.py e15` runs one idea).

Scope rule as in RED_TEAM_IDEAS.md: models of attacks on our own link, each ending in a defensive lesson. No RF, no guidance for real targets.

## Shared spatial / radio model (_common.py, _spatial.py) - every constant is an assumption
Log-distance path loss, 40 dB at 1 m, exponent 3 (about 2.4 GHz, cluttered); noise floor -95 dBm; packet success is a logistic in SINR (centre 6 dB, width 1 dB), a smooth stand-in for a coded-packet waterfall, NOT a measured curve; 13 hop channels; Rayleigh fading where stated. Absolute distances and dB values are illustrative; the claims are about orderings and ratios, and robustness to the path-loss exponent was checked only in E13 (exponents 2 and 4).

## Results and caveats, per idea
**E12 Placement.** Patrol path = random 4-waypoint polyline (40 points) in a 100 m arena, one base transmitter (15 dBm), a jammer (0 dBm, occupies 3 of 13 channels, so at most 3/13 of slots are affected) that must stay >= 10 m from every path point. Jammer = follower: grid search (50x50) for the position minimising path-mean delivery; defender = leader: relay (second transmitter, packet-level selection diversity) on a 12x12 grid maximising that minimum. Findings: the worst spot hugs the stand-off boundary (10.6 +/- 0.3 m from the path); the relay that wins is not near the base (36.8 +/- 3.4 m away) and a relay co-located with the base is almost useless (0.910); the path centroid is a cheap heuristic (0.967 vs 0.976 optimum). Checks that are not the optimiser's own formula: vectorised vs plain-Python loop; continuous hill-climb refinement never improves the grid optimum by more than 0.0143; the chosen worst spot re-evaluated under fading + 4 dB shadowing that the optimiser never saw still ranks above 97% of random spots and the relay gain survives (+0.097 +/- 0.016). Lesson: put the relay by the geometry of the path, not by the base, and keep margin for the stand-off boundary. Caveats: mean path loss with no obstacles; jammer power and duty are fixed so the absolute loss is small (cap 3/13 = 0.23); one patrol-path family; the jammer is oblivious to the hop pattern.

**E13 Swarm.** Same geometry; N jammers share a fixed total power (equal split), greedy sequential placement. Result: modest. At equal total power N=2..16 reaches 0.877 to 0.885 vs 0.901 for one jammer, saturating from N=4; to push delivery below 0.90 the swarm needs 1.79 +/- 0.60 dB less total power (paired). It is a real but small effect, and it appears only because the 10 m stand-off keeps one jammer from standing on the path; with stand-off 25 m the swarm advantage vanishes (paired 0.001 +/- 0.003). Path-loss exponent check: n=2 damage ratio swarm/single 0.97, n=4 1.17 (n=4 favours the swarm, n=2 does not: the sign depends on the model). Defence: after the relay best-response and a re-optimised swarm, delivery is 0.972 (N=8) vs 0.966 (N=1): one relay neutralises the swarm. Lesson: spatial diversity (a relay) beats raw power; do not expect a swarm to be a dramatic multiplier in this model. Caveats: summing powers assumes non-coherent noise-like jamming, no coordination gain, no obstacle effects.

**E14 Energy.** Jammer energy per transmitting slot = P/0.3 + 30 mW overhead (1 ms slots, so mW = uJ per slot); reactive jammer listens at 60 mW every slot and detects with prob 0.9. Search over power (41 levels), parallel radios k, duty (20 levels) for 30% delivery loss under Rayleigh fading. The optimum is interior: 6.3 +/- 0.2 dBm; full power costs 49 times more and the "mean SINR equals threshold" rule of thumb 2.5 times more. Reactive beats blind only while its listening power is below 255 +/- 1 mW. Defender pricing: each dB of margin the defender buys raises the jammer's cost, but the exchange rate falls from 0.77 to 0.51 uJ of jammer energy per uJ of extra defender energy (3 to 12 dB), i.e. always-on margin is a poor trade versus the jammer's gated duty. Independent check: slot-level replay (random hop, random jam channels, per-slot fading) hits 0.302 +/- 0.001 loss and random alternative configurations that reach the target never cost less than the optimum (both asserted). Lesson: attacks have an energy price; the jammer's cheapest point is not its maximum power. Caveats: the energy in the replay is accounted by the same cost function (only the loss is independently re-simulated); amplifier efficiency 0.3 and 30 mW overhead are invented; "defender extra energy" is a simple ARQ retransmission count and ignores latency.

**E15 Capture.** Receiver locks onto one frame at the preamble (detection SNR 2 dB). "First-lock": earliest detectable preamble wins. "Strongest-lock": re-locks to a frame at least 6 dB stronger (message-in-message). The attacker needs only to be early and detectable: against first-lock, 50% loss at -9 dBm from 25 m, where its frame is about 24 dB weaker than ours; against the re-lock receiver it needs +6.6 +/- 0.2 dBm (15.6 +/- 0.2 dB more). Near-far: at 0 dBm an early attacker at 20 m takes delivery to 0.04 (first-lock) vs 0.66 (re-lock). Loss scales with the fraction of channels covered (k=3 of 13: 0.777, k=13: 0.073 for first-lock). MC lock rate matches the independent exponential-ratio law mu_a/(mu_a + z mu_l) within 0.012, and the k/13 coverage formula matches an explicit channel simulation. Lesson: receiver design margin: enable re-lock on a much stronger frame, authenticate before committing the receiver, and note that the attack is about timing and detectability, not power. Caveats: the 1 dB grid on the 50% threshold (the +/- 0.0 for first-lock is quantisation, not a measurement of zero spread); simultaneous and early frames behave alike for the re-lock receiver because the waterfall already kills the packet at that SIR; lock rules are textbook models, not a specific chip; no frame-level timing resolution or preamble overlap.

**E16 Honeypot.** Real traffic uses 9 of 13 channels with a non-uniform preference (top two carry 0.45; a perfect jammer would take 0.427 of delivery); 4 bait channels carry decoy frames with probability rho per slot. A decayed-count frequency jammer and an EXP3 jammer (2 channels/slot) see "activity" only. No decoys: 0.595 and 0.642 delivery. rho=0.3 (1.2 decoy frames/slot): 0.982 and 0.944; rho=0.6: 0.993 and 0.983; rho=0.1: no effect (decoys not busier than the busiest real channel, 0.25). Cost: decoy traffic of 1.2 to 2.4 frames per real frame. Negative result, important: if the jammer can tell decoy from real (Q_D=1) the honeypot gives nothing (paired change 0.000), and at Q_D=0.5 it still works (0.982 / 0.935). Lesson: decoys only work while indistinguishable (same framing, same timing statistics), and they are not free; a uniformly hopping link gains nothing from them in this model because a frequency jammer gets nothing against it anyway. Caveats: the jammer's decoy discrimination is a single knob Q_D, not a mechanism; jammer jams a fixed 2 channels; the jammer is passive (does not probe), results are from 4000-slot runs; the decoy energy is counted in frames, not joules; the test file uses a 2500-slot variant of the same code (the 4000-slot numbers above come from the script).

**E17 Moving target.** 16 configurations (modulation A robust/B fast, length 32/128 B, timing offset 0..3); a feature-locking jammer (UCB1 bandit over the 16 configurations, learns from lost packets) couples with a match gain: wrong modulation -8 dB, wrong length -3 dB, wrong timing -6 dB (assumed). Jammer 5 dBm at 25 m from the receiver, link 15 dBm at 30 m. Goodput after learning: static 0.208 +/- 0.006, 2 configs 0.346, 4 configs 0.401, 8 configs 0.500, 16 configs 0.512 +/- 0.004; paired gain n=16 over static +0.304 +/- 0.006. Price: unjammed goodput 1.000 (up to 4 configs), 0.944 (8), 0.721 (16) since slow/short configs enter the mix. The benefit depends on jammer power: against an offline oracle jammer at -10 dBm the static link beats full diversity (0.85 vs 0.70, jammer too weak to matter); at 5 to 20 dBm diversity pays; at 30 dBm everything is drowned (0.00 / 0.02). Consistency checks: the PRR table equals a plain-Python per-draw implementation; the learning jammer never beats the best fixed arm by more than sampling noise. Lesson: a diversity metric (H = log2 of effective configurations) buys goodput under attack only in the middle power range and costs clean goodput; pick the 4 to 8 best configurations, not all. Caveats: the match gains are the whole story - they are assumptions and the ordering depends on them; the jammer is on the hop channel every slot (worst case); timing and length are discrete labels, not a waveform model; no channel hopping interaction; uniform mixing only (no optimised mixture).

## Failures, limits and not done
- E13: the swarm effect is small; stated as such, and it is sensitive to the stand-off (zero at 25 m).
- E16: honeypot fails against a discerning jammer (reported, asserted).
- E14/E15/E17: parameters (efficiency, detection SNR, match gains) are assumptions; no calibration against hardware. Path-loss exponent sensitivity was done for E13 only (the sign of the swarm effect is not stable across n=2 and n=4).
- Not done: Stackelberg with a relay that moves, joint placement of several relays, a reactive jammer in E12/E13, optimised (non-uniform) feature mixtures in E17, decoy discrimination as a mechanism in E16, frame-level preamble overlap in E15, scorecard integration with lab6/exp8 (the lab6 scorecard was not changed).
